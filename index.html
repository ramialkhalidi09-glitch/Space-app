Why this story

Silent is not the same as gone

For more than sixty years NASA has sent machines to places people could not reach yet. When their work ended, most of them stayed where they were. Landers sit where they touched down. Rovers stopped in the middle of a drive. Probes are still flying with their radios switched off.

Each one answered a question. Opportunity read the rocks of ancient Mars. Mirrors left by Apollo crews still measure how fast the Moon is drifting away. Pioneer 10 carries a plaque toward a star. These machines are monuments, and some of them are still working.

The Moon, 1.3 light-seconds away

The Moon keeps everything

The Moon has no wind or rain to wipe things away. Boot prints, rover tracks and landers stay exactly where they were left. Between 1966 and 1972 NASA put five robot landers and six crews on the surface. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter can still see their equipment from above.

Left behind on the way down

The parts nobody drives

A landing leaves hardware on the ground that was never meant to work after it arrived. These are the pieces from Perseverance's landing, and one piece from Opportunity's.

Heat shield

It protects a spacecraft while it plunges into the air. Opportunity photographed its own discarded heat shield in early January 2005 and drove over to study it.

Parachute and backshell

Dropped at a safe distance during the descent. On April 19, 2022, Ingenuity photographed them from the air, about 1 km (0.6 miles) northwest of the landing site.

Descent stage

This rocket platform lowered Perseverance on cables, then flew away and crashed about 2 km (1.2 miles) to the southeast, so it could not land on the rover.

NASA tracks this debris on purpose. Teams check that it does not drift into the rover's sample tubes, and they report no immediate concerns.

One machine, start to finish

The last months of Opportunity

Opportunity was built to work for 90 Martian days. This is how it spent 14 years instead, and how it ended.

Oct. 11, 2026

Opportunity's record of 28.06 miles (45.16 km) stood until Perseverance's odometer reached 28.10 miles (45.22 km). When the record fell, NASA's team remembered the rover that set it. At the end, project manager John Callas said: "Farewell, Opportunity, and well done."

    Deep space, up to one light-day away

    Out where the radio takes a day

    The farthest machines are so far away that a message takes hours to reach them. Some are still talking. Others ran out of power years ago and are still flying, still orbiting or still sitting where they landed.

    Countdown

    Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth

    On Nov. 18, 2026 at 06:16 UTC, Voyager 1 will be about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km) away. A radio message sent from Earth will then take a full 24 hours to arrive.

    It left Earth on Sept. 5, 1977. NASA expects Voyager 2 to reach the same mark in November 2035.

      About this project

      Challenge
      Abandoned but not Forgotten: Storytelling about NASA's Discarded Equipment on the Moon and Mars, from the 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge.
      Made for
      School-age space fans, roughly ages 9 to 15, and anyone else who is curious. Hard words are explained below.
      Accuracy
      Dates, distances and quotes come from NASA and the other sources listed below, checked in November 2026. The boxes marked "Imagined voice" are made up from real events and are always labelled.
      Built with

      Words you will meet

      Lander
      A spacecraft that touches down and stays where it lands.
      Rover
      A robot with wheels that drives around after it lands.
      Light-day
      The distance light travels in one day, about 25.9 billion km. A light-second and a light-minute work the same way.
      Martian day
      One turn of Mars, about 24 hours 40 minutes long. Scientists call it a sol.
      Mirror panel
      Small mirrors that send light straight back to where it came from. Scientists call them retroreflectors.
